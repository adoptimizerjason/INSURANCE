# Insurance Sales Business Plan — Las Vegas, Nevada

*Starting from scratch: Health vs. Life, Captive vs. Independent vs. Own FMO*

---

> **Disclaimer:** This is a strategic planning document, not legal, tax, or financial advice. Commission figures are 2026 industry benchmarks and CMS-published maximums; actual carrier payouts vary and change. Verify all licensing fees, carrier contracts, and CMS rules directly with the Nevada Division of Insurance and each carrier before acting. The author is not a lawyer or financial advisor.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This plan evaluates building an insurance sales practice in Las Vegas, Nevada, starting with no book of business and no prior production history. It answers four questions:

1. **Health or Life?** — Recommendation: **Both, sequenced.** Lead with Medicare/health to build a recurring renewal base, layer in life and final expense for cash velocity.
2. **Work for a company that owns the book, or build your own?** — Recommendation: **Start independent under a strong FMO** (you keep your book). Use a captive role only as a short paid apprenticeship if you have zero sales background and no runway.
3. **Should I become my own FMO?** — Recommendation: **Not at the start.** It is an endpoint reached after 2–4 years of production, not a launch strategy. Build a downline as a GA under an upline first.
4. **What does the first 12–24 months actually look like?** — Covered in Sections 8–10 with a budget, timeline, and income projections.

The single biggest strategic point: **whoever owns the renewals owns the business.** Every structural decision in this plan is filtered through that lens.

## 2. Why Las Vegas / Nevada Is a Strong Market

| Factor | Detail | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| Senior population growth | Clark County is one of the fastest-growing retirement-destination metros in the U.S. | Large, expanding Medicare-eligible pool aging in at ~10,000 nationally turning 65 daily |
| In-migration | Heavy inbound migration from California (higher-cost state) | Transplants actively shopping new coverage = warm market |
| No state income tax | Nevada has no personal state income tax | More of your commission stays with you vs. CA/NY-based agents |
| Licensing cost | Nevada is one of only three states with **no NIPR transaction fee** | Lower barrier to entry |
| Year-round need | Medicare AEP (Oct 15–Dec 7), OEP (Jan 1–Mar 31), plus life/final expense year-round | Smooths the seasonal income gap if you carry multiple product lines |

**Caveat:** Las Vegas is a saturated, heavily-marketed Medicare market. Many agents and call centers compete here. Differentiation through service, niche (e.g., dual-eligible, specific language communities), and retention matters more than in a rural market.

## 3. Licensing — Nevada Requirements & Costs

You cannot sell anything until licensed. There is **no "FMO license"** — FMO is a contractual status with carriers, not a state credential.

### 3.1 Steps (Individual Resident Producer)

1. **Pre-licensing education** — Not legally required in Nevada, but strongly recommended to pass the exam. Cost ~$40–$200 depending on provider (ExamFX, A.D. Banker, Kaplan).
2. **Pass the Nevada licensing exam** via Pearson VUE — must be passed within one year prior to applying. Single-line exam fee and combo (Life/Health) exam fee apply (combo is the efficient choice — see below).
3. **Fingerprints & background check** — Digital fingerprints required; can be completed within 90 days after submitting the application.
4. **Apply via Sircon** — State licensing fee is **$185**. Add a line of authority to an existing license for **$50**.
5. **Get appointed** — Nevada law requires every producer (individual and business entity) to be appointed by an insurance company before selling its products.
6. **Continuing education** — 30 hours every 3 years, including 3 hours of ethics.

### 3.2 Estimated All-In Licensing Budget

| Item | Estimated Cost |
|---|---|
| Pre-licensing course (Life + Health combo) | $40 – $200 |
| Exam fee (combo Life/Health) | ~$47 – $65 |
| State license fee (Sircon) | $185 |
| Fingerprints / background | ~$40 – $60 |
| **Total to get licensed** | **~$310 – $510** |

> **Recommendation:** Take the **combined Life & Health** line of authority from day one. It costs marginally more than a single line and unlocks the "sell both" strategy this plan is built around. Adding a line later costs $50 plus re-study.

### 3.3 If You Build a Business Entity (Agency)

A business entity producer license is separate from your individual license:

- Licensing fee: **$185 + $50 per Designated Responsible Licensed Producer (DRLP)**
- You (or a licensed person) must be named DRLP and be responsible for the entity's compliance
- Requires proof of incorporation (LLC/Corp registered with Nevada Secretary of State)

## 4. Health vs. Life — The Core Product Decision

### 4.1 Side-by-Side

| Dimension | Health / Medicare | Life Insurance |
|---|---|---|
| **Income shape** | Flat, renewal-weighted; repeats every year client stays enrolled | Front-loaded; large Year 1, thin renewals that taper |
| **Cash velocity** | Slower to ramp; builds an annuity-like base | Fast; a strong month pays immediately |
| **Commission cap** | Yes — CMS sets a hard Fair Market Value maximum | No government cap; contract level & product mix drive income |
| **Seasonality** | Intense (AEP Oct 15–Dec 7); OEP Jan–Mar | Year-round |
| **Regulation** | Heavy — CMS rules, mandatory annual AHIP/NABIP certification (must score ≥85%), strict marketing/recording rules | Lighter — state insurance regs, suitability, no CMS regime |
| **Book durability** | Strong — renewals carry you through lean months | Weaker trailing tail; you keep hunting |
| **Compliance/legal risk** | Elevated in 2025–2026 (DOJ kickback litigation, CMS rule uncertainty) | Lower, more stable |
| **Best for** | Predictable recurring income, service-oriented temperament | Upside, speed, tolerance for income variability |

### 4.2 2026 Medicare Commission Benchmarks (CMS Maximums — "All Other States" incl. Nevada)

> These are CMS Fair Market Value **maximums**. Carriers may pay less, never more. Renewals are capped at 50% of current FMV.

| Product | 2026 Initial (Year 1) | 2026 Renewal (Year 2+) |
|---|---|---|
| Medicare Advantage (MA) — Nevada/national base | **$694 / member / year** | **$347 / member / year** |
| Medicare Part D (PDP) | **$114 / member / year** | **$57 / member / year** |
| MA referral fee | $100 | — |
| PDP referral fee | $25 | — |

*2026 was the largest MA commission increase in years (~10.9% over 2025). Note: Medicare Supplement (Med Supp) commissions are NOT CMS-capped — they are carrier-set, typically a percentage of premium with a longer renewal tail, and are a strong complementary product.*

### 4.3 Life & Final Expense Commission Benchmarks (Industry Typical Ranges)

> Not government-capped. Vary widely by carrier, product, and your contract level.

| Product | Year 1 Commission (% of annual premium) | Renewal |
|---|---|---|
| Term life | ~50% – 100%+ | ~2–5%, limited years |
| Whole life | ~50% – 100%+ | ~2–5%, tapering |
| Final expense (simplified-issue WL) | ~80% – 120% | Modest renewals across many small policies |
| Annuities (FIA/MYGA) | ~1% – 7% one-time | Usually none unless trail-based |

### 4.4 Verdict

**Sell both — but sequence it.** Lead generation, demographic, and client base overlap heavily (seniors). The strongest long-term Las Vegas practice:

- **Medicare Advantage + Part D + Med Supp** = the recurring renewal annuity (the foundation)
- **Final expense + term/whole life** = cash velocity to fund operations while the renewal base compounds

This is not hedging — it is the structurally optimal model. A pure-life agent has volatile income; a pure-Medicare agent has a seasonal cash crunch and full CMS exposure. The combination solves both.

## 5. The Structural Decision — Work for Someone vs. Own Your Book

There are **three tiers**, not two.

### 5.1 Tier 1 — Captive / Employee (Company Owns the Book)

**Pros**
- Salary or draw; income from day one
- Training, mentorship, structured onboarding
- Company-provided leads
- Lower personal capital risk
- Sometimes benefits (health, 401k)

**Cons**
- Lowest commission payout
- Limited product shelf — you sell what they carry, which can be wrong for the client
- **You typically do NOT own your renewals or your book** — leave, and it stays with the company
- Captive non-compete clauses can restrict your next move

**When it makes sense:** Only as a **short, deliberate paid apprenticeship** if you have no sales background and no financial runway. Go in knowing you are likely trading the book for the training. Plan your exit before you start.

### 5.2 Tier 2 — Independent Under a Strong FMO/Upline *(Recommended Starting Point)*

**Pros**
- 1099 independent; **you own your book and renewals**
- Full product shelf — multiple carriers, choose what's right for the client
- Keep street-level commissions (a good FMO is paid by the carrier override, **not** out of your commission)
- FMO provides contracting, training, technology, CRM, lead programs, back-office support
- You can build toward your own downline later from this position

**Cons**
- No salary — you fund your own ramp
- Support quality varies enormously between FMOs (vet hard — see Section 6)
- Requires self-direction and discipline
- You carry your own E&O, marketing, and lead costs

**When it makes sense:** **For almost everyone starting from scratch with any sales ability and 3–6+ months of runway.** This is the recommended path.

### 5.3 Tier 3 — Build Your Own FMO (Direct Top-Level Carrier Contracts)

**Pros**
- Capture the override on your own production
- Earn override on **every downline agent you recruit** — income scales beyond your personal selling hours
- The only structure where income compounds independent of your time
- This is how the large incomes in the industry are actually built

**Cons**
- Carriers grant top-level contracts on a **production promise** — often a commitment in the range of **several million dollars in annual production** — plus a track record
- **Liability for your downline's conduct** — if an agent takes advances and never places business, the FMO repays it
- Real fixed costs: staff, technology, lead funding, per-carrier/per-state license fees, E&O, often advancing commissions before overrides arrive → working-capital and cash-flow risk
- You stop being primarily a salesperson — it becomes recruiting, training, compliance, and management. Many strong producers dislike this and earn less doing it.
- Medicare-specific: 2025–2026 DOJ kickback litigation and CMS rule uncertainty raise compliance exposure for organizations paying downlines

**When it makes sense:** As a **deliberate later business decision** once you have the production history, downline relationships, and capital — and genuinely want to run a distribution company rather than sell.

### 5.4 The Recommended Sequence

```
Stage 0 (optional): Captive apprenticeship  — only if zero sales background + no runway
        │  (skip if you can)
        ▼
Stage 1: Independent under a strong FMO     — keep your book, full product shelf, 12–24 months
        │
        ▼
Stage 2: Build a small downline as a GA     — override economics, lower direct exposure,
        │  under your existing upline           still no carrier production commitments
        ▼
Stage 3: Your own direct FMO contracts      — ONLY if you want to run distribution
                                               and can credibly promise carrier volume
```

**Why this order:** Carriers will not grant top-level FMO contracts without a production track record. Going straight for your own FMO with no history usually means you cannot get the contracts — and if you somehow could, you would be undercapitalized and over-exposed to downline liability.

## 6. How to Vet an FMO (Stage 1 Make-or-Break)

Not all FMOs are equal. Before signing, get written answers:

| Question | Why It Matters |
|---|---|
| What contract level (street-level or above) do you assign me? | Determines your actual commission |
| **Do I own my book? Is it vested? What happens if I leave?** | The single most important question — get it in writing |
| Are there release provisions? How hard is it to leave for another FMO? | "Captivity by contract" — some FMOs make leaving punitive |
| What carriers can I contract with through you? | Product shelf breadth = ability to serve clients |
| What leads do you provide — free, shared, exclusive, cost? | Lead economics make or break year one |
| What training, CRM, and back-office support is included? | Real value of the override they earn |
| Do you take any cut of my personal commission? | A good FMO does not — they are paid by carrier override |
| Bonuses, contests, trips, production incentives? | Legitimate added value |

> **Red flags:** No written vesting/book-ownership terms; pressure to sign immediately; taking a cut of your personal commission; vague or punitive release language; no real training or lead support.

## 10. 12–24 Month Action Timeline

| Phase | Timeframe | Actions |
|---|---|---|
| **Phase 1 — Get licensed** | Months 0–1 | Pre-licensing course (Life+Health combo); pass Pearson VUE exam; fingerprints; apply via Sircon ($185); form LLC |
| **Phase 2 — Contract & equip** | Months 1–2 | Vet and join a strong FMO (Section 6); contract with 4–8 carriers; bind E&O; complete AHIP/NABIP; set up CRM |
| **Phase 3 — Produce** | Months 2–12 | Focus 100% on personal production both lines; learn compliance cold; build referral pipeline; track every renewal |
| **Phase 4 — Evaluate scaling** | Months 12–24 | Assess: do you want to recruit? If yes, build a small downline as a GA under your upline (Stage 2). If no, deepen personal book |
| **Phase 5 — FMO decision** | Year 2–4 | Only if you have production volume + want to run distribution: pursue direct top-level carrier contracts (Stage 3) |

## 11. Risk Register

| Risk | Likelihood | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| No income during ramp (no salary) | High | 3–6 month personal runway reserve before starting independent |
| Choosing a bad FMO (book not owned) | Medium | Vet hard; get vesting/release terms in writing (Section 6) |
| Medicare regulatory/legal shift (DOJ litigation, CMS rule changes) | Medium | Diversify into life/final expense + Med Supp; stay strictly compliant; carry multiple carriers |
| Seasonal cash gap (post-AEP) | High | Carry life/final expense for year-round income |
| Chargebacks (rapid disenrollment, advanced commissions clawed back) | Medium | Maintain a chargeback reserve; avoid over-reliance on advances |
| Market saturation in Las Vegas | High | Differentiate via niche, service, retention; referral-based growth |
| Over-extending into own FMO too early | Medium | Follow the staged sequence; do not skip to Stage 3 |

## 12. Bottom-Line Recommendations

1. **Product:** Get the **Life + Health combo license**. Lead with **Medicare (MA + PDP + Med Supp)** for the renewal base; layer **final expense and life** for cash velocity. Do not pick one — the combination is the strategy.
2. **Structure:** Start **independent under a rigorously vetted FMO** so you own your book. Use captive only as a short paid apprenticeship if you have no sales background and no runway — and know you are likely giving up the book for the training.
3. **Own FMO:** **Not now.** It is a Stage 3 endpoint. Build production and a downline as a GA first. Pursue direct carrier FMO contracts only when you can promise the volume and genuinely want to run a distribution business.
4. **Capital:** Budget ~$3K–$11K startup plus **3–6 months personal runway**. The renewal book — not Year 1 gross — is the real prize; protect its ownership above everything else.
